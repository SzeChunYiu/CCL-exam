# How the reference CCL dialogues are constructed

This note is based on the supplied Cantonese CCL practice samples and is used as a writing specification for this practice bank.

## What the question writer is testing

The dialogues do not read like vocabulary quizzes. They create a believable Australian community-service problem in which the interpreter must preserve **meaning, intent, register, numbers, conditions and consequences** while switching direction repeatedly.

## Recurring characteristics in the samples

- A concrete problem appears early: a payment, appointment, application, repair, entitlement, complaint or deadline.
- Each speaker has a reason for speaking. The client is often worried, uncertain or trying to make a decision; the service provider is clarifying facts and explaining an actionable next step.
- Turns vary in length. Short acknowledgements and clarification questions sit beside information-dense turns, rather than every segment having the same rhythm.
- Difficulty often comes from ordinary details: dates, money, reference numbers, timing, eligibility conditions, documents, exceptions and cause/effect.
- Australian terminology appears only when the case naturally needs it. The surrounding sentence still sounds like a real conversation.
- The dialogue moves forward. Later turns depend on information established earlier instead of repeating the same procedural template.
- A realistic resolution closes the problem: what to submit, whom to contact, what to keep, what not to do, or what happens next.

## Cantonese style

The reference material uses **spoken Cantonese**, not Mandarin-shaped written Chinese. Natural features include forms such as `我哋`, `咁`, `點樣`, `係咪`, `唔`, `喺`, `畀`, and sentence-final particles where appropriate. The aim is natural Hong Kong Cantonese while preserving the original speaker's level of formality.

## What this bank deliberately avoids

- learner-facing lines such as “remember this term” or “the key expression is…” inside an exam dialogue;
- a compulsory appeal/review question at the end of every scenario;
- identical identity checks, document-upload questions or processing-time questions repeated across many topics;
- forcing an Australian acronym into every turn;
- exactly the same number and length of segments in every dialogue;
- third-person interpreting prompts such as “tell her that…”.

## V3 writing gates

The final bank contains **100 dialogues and 1,412 alternating source segments**. Dialogues contain **12–16 segments**, are **272–324 English-equivalent words**, and no source segment exceeds **35 English-equivalent words**. Exact full-turn repetition is capped at **four occurrences** across the entire bank.

The model interpretations are examples of acceptable meaning transfer, not scripts that must be memorised word-for-word.
